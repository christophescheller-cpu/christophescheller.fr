THE NOVEL

Can a story leave a profound mark on us when it barely had time to exist?

Christophe no longer expected to meet someone who could turn his life upside down.

Then came Juliana.

A few days in Paris. An unexpected sense of connection. And soon, the distance between Paris and Brasília, the silences, the waiting, and the strange impossibility of fully living a story that nevertheless refuses to fade.

Because some encounters never become a life together. They become part of us.

Le Goût des Fraises Sauvages tells the story of what remains when a relationship ends but what it awakened lives on.

Through memory, longing, desire, letting go and rebuilding, Christophe gradually tries to understand why some people pass through our lives for only a few moments yet leave a lasting mark.

An intimate novel about love, absence and those encounters that sometimes force us to see who we were — and who we might still become — in a different light.

Inspired by a true story, Le Goût des Fraises Sauvages explores the fragile boundary between what we actually lived, what we hoped to live, and what remains despite everything.

The first glanceAct I — The encounter

A glance, a smile, then the desire to speak to this stranger.

Then Christophe looked up.

He saw her.

Her light brown hair, touched with lighter strands, fell freely over her shoulders. A delicate face, dark, very expressive eyes.

She was beautiful.

But it was not really her beauty that held his gaze.

It was her smile.

A broad, spontaneous smile that seemed to spread across her whole face, all the way to her eyes.

She was talking to her friend, sometimes accompanying her words with a gesture of her hand. Then she listened. Laughed. Became attentive again.

She seemed unaware that anyone might be watching her.

And yet Christophe was watching her.

Christophe looked away.

A few seconds later, without even realising it, he was looking at her again.

Hassan was still talking about the breakup he could not understand.

Christophe was listening.

Or at least trying to.

His gaze kept returning to her.

There was something deeply alive about this woman. A way of being wholly present, in a conversation, in laughter, in the moment.

He knew nothing about her.

Not her name.

Not her age.

Not the city where she lived.

Not even the language she was speaking.

He knew nothing of the life behind that smile.

For now, all he saw was a woman sitting a few tables away.

And a smile that, for a reason he did not yet understand, kept him from looking anywhere else for long.

One thing eventually became clear to him.

He did not want to leave this restaurant without speaking to her.

He stood up.

Without a plan.

Without really knowing what he was going to say.

When he reached her table, Juliana looked up at him.

For the first time, their eyes truly met.

Up close, Christophe found again the smile that had held his gaze. But he also discovered her eyes, lively, curious, almost amused to see this stranger standing before her table.

He hesitated for a second.

“Excuse me… I’d like to keep a memory of this evening. Would you mind if I took your photograph?”

Juliana seemed surprised.

There was clearly something unexpected about the request.

Then she smiled again.

“Of course.”

Christophe took the photograph.

He could have thanked her and returned to his table.

He did not.

A few words led to others.

He learned that she was Brazilian, that she came from Brasília and was in Paris for a training placement with the fire service. She told him about her work, her stay, the city she was discovering with such enthusiasm.

She would be leaving in three days.

Three days.

Christophe looked at her friend, then turned back to Juliana.

“And your husband? Is he a firefighter too?”

Before Juliana even had time to answer, her friend burst out laughing.

“No, she’s single!”

Juliana rolled her eyes with a smile, caught between amusement and embarrassment.

Christophe laughed too.

“Well… so am I.”

This time, they looked at each other for a few seconds longer.

Nothing spectacular.

Just a smile exchanged between two strangers in a Parisian restaurant.

Christophe asked for her number so he could send her the photograph.

She gave it to him.

Everything had been astonishingly simple.

The questions an encounter leaves behindPrologue

Time and memory sometimes change the meaning of a story.

There are stories we live through, and others we understand only afterwards.

Some seem to end one evening, at a station, an airport, or in a final message.

Yet sometimes they go on living long after they have ended.

They shift slowly in memory.

Return without warning.

Change their face with time.

Until one day, a question we thought we had put to rest presents itself differently.

Christophe and Juliana’s story belongs to that category.

When they met in Paris, nothing seemed likely to bring them together.

They lived on two continents.

Two lives already built.

Two lives filled with work, responsibilities and the fragile balances that age eventually imposes.

Chance gave them only a few days in the same city.

A few days that might have been no more than a brief interlude among so many others.

They were not.

For a long time, Christophe believed this story had already revealed its meaning to him.

He thought he knew where it had begun.

Why it had ended.

What it had left behind.

He was wrong.

Some truths emerge only once silence has returned.

It sometimes takes a long time to understand what a few weeks have truly changed.

And longer still to discover that the questions we were asking may not have been the right ones.

That is where this story begins.

What if Juliana came back?Act VII — Maturity

Some questions do not seek to predict the future. They help us see what has changed within us.

What if Juliana came back?

For a long time, the answer would have seemed obvious to him.

He would have gone back to her without hesitation.

He would have returned to the path he had never truly left in his heart.

Back then, only one thing mattered.

Loving her.

Today, though, the answer no longer seemed so simple.

Not because he no longer loved her.

Love had never been the question.

But Christophe had come to understand that his love for Juliana had not been nourished only by the woman she was.

It had also been nourished by the love he had believed he received from her.

By her words.

By the place she made for him in her life.

By the way she had believed in them.

He had loved Juliana. And he had deeply loved being loved by her.

That certainty had nourished his own love far more than he had understood at the time.

It had given their story a sense of inevitability.

It had turned an extraordinary encounter into a possible future.

And something had broken there too.

Something Christophe had taken a long time to accept.

The wound.

For months, he had searched for the reason their story had ended.

He had tried to understand Juliana.

Her fears.

Her contradictions.

The choices she might have faced.

He had learned to accept that a story could be profoundly true and yet find no place in a life.

But understanding that did not erase what had happened.

Then he imagined what that return might look like.

Not necessarily in Paris.

Not at his door.

Simply in his life.

If one day her name appeared on his phone again.

If, behind that name, he heard again the voice he knew by heart.

And if she simply said to him:

“Chris... I still love you. I know you're the one.”

For a few seconds, everything he thought he had understood fell away.

The analyses.

The hypotheses.

The answers patiently pieced together.

Only one feeling remained.

Immediate.

Almost untouched.

The heart has its own memory.

Sometimes it recognises someone before thought has even begun its work.

Christophe knew that.

Something within him would recognise Juliana.

Her smile.

Her voice.

That tenderness he had loved so much.

Perhaps even that light he had thought he saw again whenever she appeared on his screen.

And for a few seconds, perhaps he would simply want to take her in his arms.

But he now knew that another memory would awaken almost at once.

The memory of February 4.

And with it, a question that had never existed before.

Could he still believe in her love in the same way?

Not because he would think Juliana was lying.

Perhaps that was precisely what made the question so difficult.

She could already have loved him sincerely.

She could already have sincerely believed in their future.

And yet she had left.

Christophe had come to understand that love and the possibility of living that love were not always the same thing.

That understanding had helped him see their story differently.

It had even allowed him to imagine that Juliana’s decision might not have been a rejection of him. That perhaps she had simply been unable to choose the life they would have needed to build to live that love.

But that same understanding also changed what a possible return would mean.

Once, hearing I love you would have been enough.

Today, Christophe could still believe her.

But believing her would no longer be enough.

SynopsisThe complete story of the novel

In Le Goût des Fraises Sauvages, I tell the story of a mutual love that opens up the possibility of a shared life, and then of how its ending continues to transform the person left behind. Memory, writing and the reconsideration of an earlier relationship accompany this journey. But understanding differently means neither knowing the other person’s whole truth nor erasing the wound.

Christophe leads a balanced life in Paris, rebuilt after his divorce, between his work as an engineer, his son, his friends and squash. At a dinner with his former wife’s new husband, himself facing a separation, he notices Juliana, a Brazilian military firefighter visiting France for a training placement. Her smile prompts him to speak to her. Only a few days remain before her return to Brasília. They exchange messages, decide to spend an evening together, then part at the airport after a kiss that gives their encounter an unexpected depth.

Distance does not bring this interlude to a close. For almost three months, their messages, calls and photographs build a daily presence. They tell each other about their work, their families, their worries and the small events of their days. Christophe discovers Juliana’s faith, her tenderness, her courage and the central place of her daughter Esther. Juliana confides her vulnerabilities to him, tells him she feels safe with him and takes an active part in their future. Their love also draws strength from this shared experience: loving and knowing oneself to be loved.

Meeting again becomes a concrete plan. Christophe books his ticket for February. Juliana prepares to welcome him, arranges her apartment, learns French and imagines the places they will discover together. They discuss other trips, the possibility of marriage and a family. Without yet having shared everyday life under the same roof, they begin to inhabit a shared life inwardly. The narrative even shows Christophe arriving in Brasília, the embrace in the arrivals hall and the apartment door opening. Then his awakening reveals that the scene was a dream: the trip has not yet happened.

On February 4, a few days before the planned departure, Juliana tells him they cannot be a couple. For Christophe, nothing in their recent exchanges had prepared him for such a sudden ending. The woman who was still arranging his visit seems to have become the one excluding him from that future. He understands the words of the message but cannot connect this decision to the story they were building together. The breakup destroys a plan, a certainty of being chosen and the coherence of his inner world all at once.

Yet they continue for a while to take care of each other. Juliana considers welcoming him as a friend; Christophe tries to believe they could preserve their encounter in that way. Both eventually recognise that, after what they have experienced, this friendship cannot give the trip a bearable form. Christophe cancels it. The imagined future disappears, while sleepless nights, tears and everyday reflexes make its absence felt. Messages grow less frequent. A few gestures of affection remain, notably on his birthday, before silence occupies an ever greater place.

Christophe rereads their conversations. He tries to understand how Juliana could see him as the man of her life while doubting the life they could actually build. The need for a presence beside her and Esther, responsibilities and distance become more visible to him. He begins to see that sincere love may fail to become a life choice. Yet this understanding remains incomplete: it does not explain with certainty what happened between the day before and the day of the breakup.

He begins writing to preserve the traces of their story. Gradually, the narrative allows him to move through it differently and to examine his own way of loving: his tendency to give possibilities the substance of memories. He sends his manuscript to Juliana. In the novel, her reply brings another voice. She affirms the reality of her love, says she felt seen and describes the loneliness that returned after their conversations. Her perspective shifts Christophe’s without offering an exhaustive explanation of the separation or of the silence that follows.

An earlier confidence from Juliana about a relationship preceding their encounter then awakens the memory of Karima. Many years before, Christophe had loved this woman while another future was already taking shape in his life. Unable to bring his feelings and his choices into alignment, he had let her go. This memory places him on the other shore of separation. He discovers what it can cost to give up a love that is still alive and considers what his own silence may have left with the other person. The two stories illuminate each other, but Karima’s never becomes proof of what Juliana might have experienced.

Christophe considers several hypotheses: the weight of distance, fear of stepping into reality, another relationship, or the inability to choose the life they imagined. None is his to claim as a certainty. He also learns not to reduce Juliana to a single face. The loving woman who made room for him and the woman whose decision and subsequent silence wounded him are the same woman. Acknowledging the beauty of their love no longer obliges him to soften the violence of its ending.

Life begins to draw his gaze again. One evening, after laughing with friends and enjoying a smile exchanged with a stranger, Christophe wonders what he would do if Juliana returned. The feeling could be rekindled; trust would not return through the force of a declaration alone. Asked whether he could entrust his heart to her again, his present answer is no. But that no does not determine what he would do in a real encounter. Time, explanations and the possibility of looking together at what was broken would be needed. Christophe closes the hypothesis: he no longer has to answer today on behalf of the man he would be then.

The epilogue leaves longing, disappointment and occasional anger at the silence intact. Yet Christophe can lay this story down in the book without making it false or asking Juliana to be different from who she was. The memory of the wild strawberries of his childhood gives this lasting trace an image: a rare flavour that lingers without being reproducible. The encounter mattered. It does not need to have lasted to have been true, nor to be fully explained to find a place in his life.

Critical self-assessmentFour perspectives on the writing and construction of the book
Style: language and formal choices

I sought a simple language, close to the gestures and words through which a relationship is built. Dinner, a shared dessert, a box of macarons or photographs sent during the day give love a concrete presence. I wanted readers to understand why this encounter matters before the character begins analysing its consequences.

Short sentences and numerous isolated paragraphs are a highly visible formal choice. They slow certain moments, draw out a word and make perceptible the work of a thought advancing through successive returns. In the breakup scene, this fragmentation accompanies shock. The message arrives in pieces; Christophe rereads it because his understanding of the words has not yet caught up with what is happening to him. The rhythm is therefore not simply slow. It can establish tenderness, suspend an expectation or convey an abrupt upheaval.

Exchanges in French, English and Portuguese are also part of this novelistic language. I preserve their simplicity and sometimes their awkwardness because these contribute to intimacy. The words do not circulate between abstract voices: they seek a common language. “Saudades”, terms of endearment and learning French show how two people try to bring their worlds closer. The telephone thus becomes a narrative space, first inhabited by presence, then marked by notifications that no longer arrive.

Repetition accompanies shifts in meaning. The airport, the apartment door, morning coffee and the same phrases from Juliana return in different lights. The anticipated scene in Brasília becomes a dream, then the trace of a lost future. Words that once reassured can later awaken pain or support another interpretation. Rather than repeating an emotion, I seek to show how memory revisits the same elements without making exactly the same story of them.

However, I can no longer describe this writing as entirely free of commentary or psychological analysis. The narrative contains long passages of reflection, explicit questions and statements of general scope. As Christophe writes, his thought becomes a central substance of the novel. Formulation, contrast and questioning do more than connect scenes: they give them direction. This choice allows us to follow his inner shift precisely, but demands vigilance about the space left to readers.

The recurring use of “perhaps”, “for a long time” and certain contrasting constructions serves a purpose: it makes hesitation, duration and the revision of certainties felt. Their accumulation also carries a risk of monotony or insistence. Likewise, isolated sentences can give every proposition comparable weight when they do not all have the same importance. My standard is to maintain a distinction between a return that transforms meaning and one that merely restates what has already been understood.

The third person allows me to transform personal material into narrative. It does not remove emotional proximity to Christophe, but avoids entirely conflating his voice with mine. The distance remains fragile: the narrator often follows his reasoning and can express his conclusions forcefully. I therefore need to preserve the markers that locate those conclusions within a consciousness, especially when they concern Juliana.

Today I place this work on language at the meeting point of sensory narrative and meditation. Its purpose is to hold together the experience of love and that of the wound, without turning reflection into a demonstration that simplifies them. The restraint I seek lies as much in caution about certainties as in simple vocabulary.

Themes and values: literary, philosophical and symbolic concerns

I wanted to tell the story of a love in which two people truly took part. Juliana does not merely receive Christophe’s impulse: she proposes, prepares, reassures and imagines a life with him. This reciprocity is essential because it gives the breakup its depth. Christophe loses a woman he loves, but also the experience of feeling loved and chosen by her.

The novel gradually distinguishes feeling, the choice of a life and the trust needed to inhabit it together. These dimensions can come together without being equivalent. Loving does not guarantee that one can rearrange family or professional life. Believing in a future does not yet mean being able to choose it. And believing in the other person’s sincerity is not always enough to feel safe with them again after a breakup.

I do not want to turn this distinction into a certain explanation for Juliana’s departure. It offers Christophe a possible understanding. Distance, loneliness, responsibilities, another relationship or fear of what the trip would make real belong to his exploration. The narrative does not give him the power to decide which of these hypotheses contains the whole truth. Accepting that limit is part of his transformation.

The wound also lies in how the separation is experienced. They built the future together; Christophe receives its ending as a decision already made without him. His anger and the feeling of sometimes having been used are realities of his experience. I let them exist without turning them into a verdict on Juliana. The word “used” says something about Christophe’s pain; it does not demonstrate an intention to manipulate.

This distinction involves a value I hold dear: recognising the other person’s freedom without making one’s own suffering illegitimate. Juliana has the right to choose her life and set a boundary. Christophe can accept that right while remaining wounded by the abrupt upheaval and the silence. His conception of love includes a responsibility towards the trust that has been placed in it. The novel explores this demand from his position without turning it into a right to determine Juliana’s future.

Silence changes meaning throughout the narrative. It can be intimacy without words, a slowing of exchanges, then a distance experienced as erasure. In Karima’s story, it also carries Christophe’s inability to choose and to speak. I do not assign it a single meaning. Even when he imagines that Juliana’s silence might protect the life she has chosen, he acknowledges that he does not know. That possibility removes neither his disappointment nor his anger.

Juliana’s two faces then become a major concern. Christophe can no longer preserve only the luminous woman of the first months, but neither does he want to reduce her to the woman who wounded him. He learns to make room for a person who remains partly contradictory to him. The beauty of the story and the violence of its ending can be acknowledged together. In this coexistence, I see a form of truthfulness more demanding than either idealisation or condemnation.

Memory does not look only towards the past. Christophe also mourns a future: places, habits and a family that had already found a place within him. The dreamed scene in Brasília gives substance to this “memory of the future”. He loses a world he never lived in, but whose absence genuinely affects his present. This tendency reveals both his capacity for wholehearted hope and his vulnerability.

Karima allows him to recognise that he himself loved without managing to choose the life that love opened up. This reconsideration does not establish a moral equivalence between two stories. It shifts his position and leads him to examine what his silence may have cost. It also reminds him that a bond can change its language, as their later meeting with their children shows, without becoming the obligatory model for what should remain with Juliana.

The scene “What if Juliana came back?” extends this reflection through a new boundary. His heart could recognise the woman he loved; life could not simply resume where it broke off. The no expresses his present inability to entrust his heart to her again with the same sense of safety. Yet it leaves open the question of what might one day be rebuilt. Above all, I wanted Christophe to stop writing an answer for a future that does not exist.

Finally, writing and the title give form to what remains. The wild strawberries come from an explicitly narrated childhood memory: small, rare fruits whose flavour persists. This image does not transform suffering into a necessary benefit. Christophe’s gratitude is directed towards the encounter, never towards the separation. The book can hold what it revealed without claiming that every wound has disappeared.

Intertextuality: affinities, references and literary traditions

I did not conceive Le Goût des Fraises Sauvages as an extension of a particular work. The affinities I can recognise concern above all a way of making time, memory and inner life into the substance of a novel. I prefer to present them as affinities of approach rather than claim influences the text would not allow me to establish.

Memory acts less as an archive to be restored than as an activity. A preserved sentence, a photograph or a gesture takes on new meaning when reread from another moment in life. The taste of wild strawberries connects a childhood sensation with this persistence of experience. The title thus finds its grounding in the novel itself, without needing to make it proof of a tribute to another work.

I also recognise myself in a tradition of love stories attentive to the difference between the intensity of an encounter and its possibility of lasting. The narrative draws on familiar situations: an unexpected meeting, departure, letters or messages, anticipated reunion. But the journey never takes place. Waiting becomes an experience to be told in its own right, and the character’s development gradually takes the place of the romantic resolution.

The messages introduce a contemporary epistolary dimension. They build intimacy, give plans an everyday reality, then become the traces Christophe questions. Juliana’s response to the manuscript opens up a space of speech different from his. This voice exists within the novel’s construction; I do not present it as an external document guaranteeing the biographical truth of the narrative. It conveys a perspective without abolishing all opacity.

Finally, the novel engages in reflection on narrative itself. Christophe begins by preserving a story and discovers that telling it transforms the person who remembers. This dimension brings the book close to a literature in which personal experience becomes a question of form and knowledge. Yet it is not about escaping lived experience through a theory of fiction: lost sleep, gestures without a recipient and anger remain the concrete ground of this reflection.

Karima’s story is above all an echo within the book. Two experiences distant in time answer each other and alter their respective meanings. This dialogue belongs to the novel’s construction rather than to an external reference. It allows an exploration of the two shores of separation while reminding us that similarities between situations never suffice to know the other person’s life.

I thus place my work in a literature of intimacy that accepts thinking explicitly about experience without claiming to resolve it. Its ambition is to offer readers situations, voices and questions to which their own memory can respond. The book’s reach depends on this possible encounter more than on its connection to a school or an author’s name.

Narratology: narrative voice, time and the construction of meaning

I chose a narrator outside the story, in the third person, with a focalisation mainly attached to Christophe. Readers follow his perceptions, emotions and shifts in interpretation. This proximity is decisive during the breakup: the narrative conveys what he cannot reconcile rather than immediately presenting an explanation from above the characters.

Yet the narrator is not simply a silent observer. He comments, offers general propositions and accompanies the character’s thought at length. Some sentences approach his inner voice; others broaden his experience. This presence gives the novel its meditative dimension. It also requires distinguishing what is experienced, what is asserted by a character and what remains a hypothesis about the other person.

Juliana exists first through what Christophe sees of her, their exchanges and their shared scenes. Her message after reading the manuscript introduces a more extended opportunity to speak. This change does not establish omniscience: someone recounting their experience does not necessarily tell everything about their life. Subsequent questions, particularly in the epilogue, maintain the gap between words received and complete knowledge of her reasons.

The progression is organised into four parts. “The birth of something undeniable” builds love between Paris and Brasília. “The turning point” conveys the breakup, the cancelled trip and the disappearance of the imagined future. “The man who remains” shifts the question towards the trace left within Christophe and towards writing. The fourth part, “The Taste of Wild Strawberries”, welcomes Juliana’s perspective, the memory of Karima and the final reconsiderations. The prologue announces that meaning will come after events; the epilogue shows what this understanding can still leave unresolved.

The chronology of the relationship provides an axis, but the novel does not rely on an entirely linear succession of events. Karima’s story is an extended return to an earlier relationship. Other memories, notably their later meeting with their children, are revisited according to the needs of present understanding. Certain scenes are therefore told when they become illuminating rather than in the place they would occupy in a biography.

The Brasília sequence plays a particular role. It is first narrated with the sensory precision of an event that has taken place. The awakening then forces readers to revise its status. This arrival is neither an actual reunion nor an announcement of an episode to come: it is the dreamed scene of a future in which Christophe already believes. The device lets readers experience the substance of that possibility before showing its loss.

This scene prepares a formal relationship with “What if Juliana came back?”. At first, imagination fulfils a life before it exists. Later, Christophe examines a possible return but refuses to go so far as to decide what follows. The final hypothesis therefore does not fill in the future: it measures what has changed in the present. The no concerning trust and the refusal to predict an actual response have different functions, which I want to preserve.

Narrative duration is distributed between closely observed scenes, summaries of weeks and long passages of reflection. The contrast between the three months of love and the time devoted to their aftermath indicates where the focus gradually lies. After the breakup, the narrative progresses through rereadings. The same messages become supports for a new thought; they do not supply an additional fact each time.

Karima’s story is not the missing piece of an investigation into Juliana. It transforms Christophe’s position: the person left behind recognises the man who could not choose and who let someone go. The comparison opens a human understanding while keeping the situations distinct. I do not want readers to take this mirroring as a demonstration of Juliana’s hidden motives.

Closure rests on a changed relationship with uncertainty. The character does not resolve every cause of the breakup, reunite with Juliana or promise restored trust. He can let the two faces of the woman he loved coexist and acknowledge his own wound. This ending seems consistent with the narrative construction: a consciousness can learn to live with what it does not know without the narrator artificially supplying the answer.

Finally, the construction requires leaving room for readers’ freedom despite the importance of commentary. Open questions alone are not enough to guarantee that freedom if every scene is followed by its interpretation. I therefore recognise the tension between the precision of Christophe’s journey and the risk of guiding readers too strongly. The plurality of hypotheses and the persistence of anger in the epilogue are essential if peace is not to become an imposed explanation.

General conclusionWhat writing helped me understand

As I complete Le Goût des Fraises Sauvages, I better understand what I was trying to preserve: a whole story. A love in which two people believed, a future they began to imagine, a breakup experienced with profound violence and the silence that eventually distanced them. Christophe’s transformation does not require choosing only one of these realities.

Writing led me to distinguish more clearly what love allows us to feel, what a life can accommodate and what trust allows us to entrust to another. These questions touch but do not resolve one another. Understanding that Juliana may have loved sincerely is not enough to know her choices. Recognising her right to leave takes nothing away from Christophe’s wound. Imagining that a life might have been different does not allow us to say what it would have been.

The memory of Karima gives this work a depth Christophe could not find by questioning Juliana alone. He also encounters his own limits, his silences and the consequences of his inability to choose. This mirror opens his perspective without giving him authority over the other person’s. I want understanding to remain an approach, sometimes fruitful, never a possession of Juliana’s truth.

For me, the scene of a possible return marks an essential stage. Christophe knows that love would no longer be enough to make trust intact. He can name this limit without turning his present no into a final decision about an unknown situation. By closing the hypothesis, he stops inwardly living a future on behalf of those who would one day have to choose it. He accepts that certain answers cannot be written in advance.

The ending therefore removes neither longing nor anger. The silence can still seem cruel to him; the encounter can remain precious. I did not want gratitude to erase pain, nor pain to make their shared love retrospectively false. The book becomes the place where these two truths can remain together without imposing an explanation on Juliana that she has not given.

Fiction allows me to give form to this experience and offer it to readers without reducing the novel to a record of facts. It does not repair the life that never happened. It makes legible the journey of a man learning to recognise what he lived, what he imagined and what he may never know. On that condition, the flavour of wild strawberries can endure: as the trace of an encounter that mattered, whose meaning can still evolve without the future already being written.

THROUGH THE REWRITING

The latest revisions to the novel

Since the originally published version, I have extensively reworked Le Goût des Fraises Sauvages. I wanted to bring the story more fully to life and explore Christophe’s experience in greater depth, while leaving more room for contradictions and unanswered questions.

I began by developing the characters and scenes. We learn more about Christophe’s life before the encounter, his habits and his sense of balance. Juliana and Karima also become more vivid through their gestures, expressions and ways of being. My aim was to help readers get to know them better and feel what draws Christophe to each of them.

Above all, I expanded the development of his relationship with Juliana: their everyday exchanges, the place they make for each other in their lives, their families, learning French, travel preparations and plans for the future. I wanted to show more clearly why Christophe had believed in this story, and how both of them had made it real despite the distance.

This led me to explore the breakup and its consequences more deeply. The pain is more tangible: the difficult nights, the tears, the cancelled trip, the future he no longer knows how to replace. I also allowed his anger, disappointment and difficulty understanding the silence to emerge. I wanted his journey towards peace to make real room for the wound.

The story with Karima has also been reworked. Christophe’s indecision is more apparent: unable to choose between two possible lives, he eventually lets her leave without trying to hold her back. This passage gives greater weight to his own responsibility and sheds a different light on how he sees Juliana: he too knows how difficult it is to give up one life in order to choose another.

Finally, I revised the ending to make Christophe more cautious in his interpretations. He considers several reasons for Juliana’s departure, but accepts that he may never know what really happened within her. His peace no longer depends so much on an explanation he needs to believe.

I also wanted to show that moving forward could mean rediscovering the pleasure of living, of looking at someone, of loving again one day, while preserving the trace of that encounter. And if Juliana were to return, feelings would no longer be enough: trust, too, would need to be rebuilt.

Through these changes, I have tried to preserve all the beauty of the story while fully acknowledging what it cost Christophe. The new version expresses this conviction more strongly: a story can have been deeply sincere without managing to become a shared life. And we can retain its value without having to soften its ending.

ABOUT THE AUTHOR

Christophe Scheller

Le Goût des Fraises Sauvages is Christophe’s first novel.

It grew out of an encounter that profoundly marked his life.

What was initially meant to be an account of a personal story became, through writing, a reflection on love, time, and the room our lives can — or cannot — make for our feelings.

How much of this story comes from lived experience? How much belongs to fiction?

Perhaps only those who recognise themselves in these pages know the answer.

The author has chosen not to answer that question.

It is for the reader to imagine where one ends and the other begins.

By the time he finished writing this first novel, Christophe had reached this conviction:

Facts belong to biography.
Truth belongs to the novel.

FROM THE WORLD OF PROJECTS TO WRITING

A book I had never thought of writing…
but that it had become impossible not to write.

For thirty years, I worked in a world where we can usually understand problems, analyse constraints and look for solutions.

Then I encountered something that could not be solved.

How can someone continue to transform your life when they are no longer part of it?

I had never imagined writing a book. I am an engineer. I spent most of my professional life analysing, managing projects, making decisions and seeking solutions.

And then there was an encounter. One that, long after it ended, kept asking me a question no method could answer.

So I began to write.

Not to publish.
Not even to tell a story.
To understand.

Little by little, memories became pages. Pages became a story. And the story became a novel.

A novel about love, memory, absence and those encounters that do not necessarily become a shared life… but can become part of us.

I do not know what you will find in this book. Perhaps a love story. Perhaps a reflection on memory and what we do with our memories. Or perhaps simply something that reminds you of someone.

Because we have probably all, at some point, met someone who changed something within us.

Today, I am happy finally to share this book with those who have known me for a long time — sometimes for several decades — but who may discover here a side of me they know less well.

READERS’ WORDS

When the book begins to belong to its readers

Writing is a solitary experience. Then comes the moment when a book leaves its author and meets its readers.

Some messages I received after readers finished Goût des Fraises Sauvages particularly moved me. They show that behind Christophe and Juliana’s story, each of us may sometimes find something of our own.

Thank you to everyone who takes the time to tell me what this story has awakened in them.
